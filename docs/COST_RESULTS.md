# Cost results (draft narrative)

Split out of the Section A narrative on 2026-08-18: **Section A is about
feasibility — how structural levers affect what is reachable.** These $/t
results come from the same design matrix but answer a different question, and
they carry a restriction Section A does not. Section placement is open.

Numbers of record: `ANALYSIS_SECTION_A.md`, generated by
`scenarios/_matrix/analyse_headlines.py` against `matrix.parquet`
(model_commit `0c3a6f2`).

---

## Two restrictions on every number here

**Calibration.** All costs are drawn from the **9,068 of 30,371 feasible
scenarios** in which the 2050 grid emission factor lies inside the calibrated
band (350–550 gCO2/kWh). Outside it the coupled industrial electricity tariff
is extrapolated past its $0.055–0.085/kWh anchors. Under a third of the
feasible design supports a cost claim, against all of it for feasibility —
which is itself an argument for reporting the two separately.

**Pairing.** Every difference is computed only over scenarios feasible at both
settings of the lever in question. Infeasibility is strongly patterned (4.8%
of scenarios at a 2030 hydrogen debut against 62.2% at 2045), so an unpaired
average would shrink every penalty by selection alone.

A third condition applies to both, and to the feasibility results: the model
charges capital as overnight expenditure in the build year and credits **no
residual value** for asset life extending past 2050. Each result below states
which way that biases it.

## The cost of delaying hydrogen is governed by the scrap supply

Against a 2030 debut, at the 1.8 target with abundant coking coal and policy
gas allocation, $/t crude steel:

| Scrap growth | 2050 scrap use | → 2035 | → 2040 | → 2045 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 37 Mt | +15.16 | infeasible | infeasible |
| 0.02 | 61 Mt | +10.08 | +41.50 * | infeasible |
| 0.04 | 99 Mt | +5.60 | +30.89 * | infeasible |
| 0.06 | 159 Mt | +1.42 | +15.71 | **+36.58** |
| 0.08 | 253 Mt | +0.01 | +3.37 | +14.33 |
| 0.10 | 394 Mt | 0.00 | +0.19 | +3.96 |

\* rests on 6 and 36 surviving pairs of 54 respectively, the remainder being
infeasible at that delay; every other cell uses all 54.

A fifteen-year delay costs 36.58 $/t at moderate scrap growth and 3.96 $/t in
a scrap-rich economy — an order of magnitude apart, driven by a lever with
nothing to do with hydrogen. Below 0.06/yr growth the delay is not priced at
all, because the target becomes unreachable (Section A).

**The deadline is not 2030.** The first five years of delay are essentially
free wherever scrap growth reaches 0.08/yr (+0.01 and 0.00 $/t) and cost 1.42
$/t at 0.06, against 36.58 $/t for the full fifteen: the cost concentrates in
the second and third five-year steps. The exception is the scrap-poor world,
where the ordering inverts and delay is expensive immediately (+15.16 $/t for
five years at zero growth) because nothing is available to absorb it.

The mechanism is displacement, not deferral — hydrogen's 2050 share falls from
50% to 11% as scrap grows (Section A). Stated without it, the finding reduces
to "delaying something you do not use is inexpensive."

**Bias: conservative.** Capital paid for but never used amounts to 13.1% of
NPV under a 2030 debut against 8.5% under a 2045 debut. The earlier programme
bears more unrecovered capital, so crediting terminal value would *widen* this
gap.

## Mandated retirement of the 2025 fleet: 38.17 $/t

Forcing a linear phase-out by 2050, rather than letting assets run their
technical lives, costs 38.17 $/t for **identical cumulative emissions** — the
cap binds either way. The cost is avoided capital expenditure, not
stranded-asset friction: forced retirement compels the model to rebuild 207.75
Mt of capacity it already owns, moving 6.3 percentage points of 2050 share out
of blast-furnace steel.

This is the study's most stable figure, varying only between 37.49 and 39.01
$/t across all four hydrogen debut years. **Bias: likely overstated**, since
the forced rebuilds land late in the horizon and receive no residual credit.

Note the pairing with Section A: retirement policy changes feasibility in 0.2%
of scenarios. It is a pure cost lever.

## Annual build capacity: 3.21 $/t early, 16.38 $/t late

The shared budget across the four conventional routes — finance and EPC
capacity, 20 to 40 Mt/yr — **never determines feasibility** (0.0% of scenarios
in the entire design). Its cost value depends sharply on hydrogen timing and
must be reported disaggregated, not averaged:

| H2 debut | Value of 20 → 40 Mt/yr |
|---|---|
| 2030 | 3.21 $/t |
| 2035 | 3.54 $/t |
| 2040 | 5.10 $/t |
| 2045 | **16.38 $/t** |

With no hydrogen route available the conventional fleet must carry the entire
transition, and the rate at which it can be rebuilt becomes the binding
industrial constraint. **The later the hydrogen programme, the more finance
and construction capacity matters.** Within any debut year, roughly 90% of the
available gain is captured by the first 10 Mt/yr.

## Electrolyser ramp

Low → medium −4.13 $/t; medium → high −2.32 $/t. Diminishing, and consistent
with the share result in Section A: hydrogen is deployment-limited at a low
ramp and approaching cost-limited at a high one.
