# Section A — results narrative (draft)

**Section A asks one question: how do structural levers affect feasibility —
what emissions targets are reachable at all, and what does reaching them
require?** Cost is not reported here. The $/t results the same table supports
are in `COST_RESULTS.md`.

Numbers of record: `ANALYSIS_SECTION_A.md` (cost) and
`analysis_section_a_feasibility.txt` (this section), both generated by
`scenarios/_matrix/analyse_feasibility.py` and `analyse_headlines.py` against
`matrix.parquet` (model_commit `0c3a6f2`). Nothing here is hand-arithmetic.

---

## Scope

The design crosses eight policy-controlled levers with three emissions
targets: coking-coal availability (2 regimes), gas allocation to steel (2),
green-H2 debut year (4: 2030–2045), scrap-availability growth (6: 0.00–0.10
/yr), 2050 grid emission factor (9: 50–850 gCO2/kWh), electrolyser deployment
rate (3), the shared annual build budget across the four conventional routes
(3: 20/30/40 Mt/yr), retirement policy for the 2025 fleet (2), and the average
emissions-intensity target (3: 1.6/1.8/2.0 tCO2/t). That is **46,656
scenarios**, each solved as an independent linear programme. **30,371 (65.1%)
are feasible**; none errored.

Feasibility uses the entire design. The calibration restriction that governs
the cost results — the coupled electricity tariff is anchored only over
350–550 gCO2/kWh — does not apply here, because extrapolating the tariff
changes what a pathway costs, not whether it exists.

The design is a **complete factorial**: every lever level appears with every
combination of the others exactly once (verified). Marginal feasibility rates
are therefore balanced by construction, and comparable across levers without
the pairing correction the cost differences require.

One caveat governs every percentage below. **"P(feasible) = 0.685" is the
share of the remaining lever combinations in the design that solve, not a
probability of real-world success.** The design weights each level of each
lever equally, which is a statement about coverage, not about likelihood.
These shares are meaningful *relative to each other* — that is what the
complete factorial buys — and should not be read as forecasts.

## Which levers decide feasibility

Two measures, both over the full design. *Swing* is the spread in P(feasible)
between a lever's most and least permissive level. *Decisiveness* is the share
of otherwise-identical scenario groups whose feasibility actually changes when
the lever moves.

| Lever | Swing | Decisive in | Least → most permissive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scrap growth | **0.611** | 61.1% | 0.00 → 0.10/yr: 0.369 → 0.981 |
| H2 debut year | **0.575** | 57.5% | 2045 → 2030: 0.378 → 0.952 |
| *(emissions target)* | *0.355* | *35.5%* | *1.6 → 2.0: 0.470 → 0.825* |
| Grid emission factor | 0.316 | 31.6% | 850 → 50 gCO2/kWh: 0.485 → 0.801 |
| Coking-coal availability | 0.123 | 12.3% | scarce → abundant: 0.589 → 0.712 |
| Electrolyser ramp | 0.123 | 12.3% | low → high: 0.584 → 0.707 |
| Gas allocation | 0.064 | 6.4% | bau → policy: 0.619 → 0.683 |
| Retirement policy | 0.002 | 0.2% | — |
| Annual build budget | **0.000** | **0.0%** | — |

The ordering is the section's main structural claim: **feasibility is decided
by how much low-carbon iron the economy can raise — scrap collection and
hydrogen timing — and only secondarily by the fossil-resource constraints that
dominate the conventional framing.** Coking-coal scarcity, the classic
import-dependence concern, moves feasibility by 0.123, a fifth of what scrap
collection does.

## The feasibility frontier: scrap and hydrogen are partial substitutes

The two dominant levers interact, and the interaction is the result. P(feasible)
across the scrap × H2-debut plane, at the strictest target (1.6 tCO2/t):

| Scrap growth | 2030 | 2035 | 2040 | 2045 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.685 | 0.120 | **0.000** | **0.000** |
| 0.02 | 0.769 | 0.202 | **0.000** | **0.000** |
| 0.04 | 0.880 | 0.370 | 0.009 | **0.000** |
| 0.06 | 0.963 | 0.611 | 0.148 | 0.009 |
| 0.08 | 1.000 | 0.926 | 0.551 | 0.258 |
| 0.10 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.949 | 0.818 |

Reading the four corners at each target:

| Target | Neither (0.00, 2045) | Hydrogen only (0.00, 2030) | Scrap only (0.10, 2045) | Both (0.10, 2030) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.6 | 0.000 | 0.685 | 0.818 | 1.000 |
| 1.8 | 0.000 | 0.898 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| 2.0 | 0.083 | 0.991 | 1.000 | 1.000 |

**Scrap and hydrogen are partial substitutes, and the substitution degrades as
the target tightens.** At 1.8 and 2.0 either lever alone is effectively
sufficient (0.898–1.000). At 1.6 neither alone is reliable — hydrogen alone
reaches 0.685, scrap alone 0.818 — while the two together reach 1.000. The
governing quantity is not either input but the *combined* low-carbon iron
supply; scrap and hydrogen are interchangeable ways of supplying it up to a
point, and past that point both are needed.

What is ruled out is unambiguous. At the 1.6 target, five (scrap, debut)
combinations are **infeasible across every configuration of the other six
levers**: hydrogen from 2040 with scrap growth at or below 0.02/yr, and
hydrogen from 2045 with growth at or below 0.04/yr. At 1.8 two such dead
slices remain; at 2.0 there are none. **No amount of grid decarbonisation,
coal availability, gas allocation, build capacity or electrolyser ramp
substitutes for a late hydrogen programme in a scrap-poor economy.**

## Grid decarbonisation matters most where hydrogen is late

The third-ranked lever is the power sector, and its effect is conditional.
P(feasible) across the grid-EF band, by hydrogen debut:

| Grid EF (gCO2/kWh) | 2030 | 2035 | 2040 | 2045 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 1.000 | 0.931 | 0.722 | 0.551 |
| 450 | 0.958 | 0.769 | 0.514 | 0.366 |
| 850 | 0.861 | 0.551 | 0.310 | 0.216 |

Cleaning the grid from 850 to 50 gCO2/kWh raises feasibility by 0.139 under a
2030 hydrogen debut and by **0.335 under a 2045 debut** — nearly 2.5 times as
much. With hydrogen available early, direct reduction carries the transition
and the grid matters less; with hydrogen late, scrap-EAF and electrified
processes carry it, and their emissions are the grid's. **Power-sector
decarbonisation is a partial hedge against a delayed hydrogen programme** —
one of the few levers that is.

## The resource constraints are real but second-order

Coking-coal availability and gas allocation move feasibility in the expected
direction and combine additively:

| | BAU gas | Policy gas |
|---|---|---|
| **Abundant coal** | 0.684 | 0.741 |
| **Scarce coal** | 0.554 | 0.625 |

The full spread from the worst resource regime to the best is 0.187 — larger
than either lever alone, smaller than the H2 debut year on its own. At the 1.6
target the same corners run 0.393 to 0.539. Import dependence constrains the
transition; it does not decide it.

## Two levers do not affect feasibility at all

**The annual build budget flips no scenario in the entire design** — 0.0% of
15,552 groups, swing 0.000 — and **retirement policy flips 0.2%**, swing
0.002. Neither the rate at which conventional capacity can be rebuilt (20 to
40 Mt/yr) nor whether the 2025 fleet is force-retired changes what is
reachable.

This is a structural finding, not an absence of one. **These are cost levers,
not feasibility levers**: both carry substantial price tags — mandated
phase-out costs 38.17 $/t and the build budget is worth 3.21–16.38 $/t
depending on hydrogen timing (see `COST_RESULTS.md`) — while changing nothing
about which targets can be met. The distinction matters for policy: they
determine what the transition costs, not whether it is available.

## Mechanism: the two low-carbon routes displace each other

The frontier's shape follows from what the routes do to one another. At a 2030
debut, 2050 production shares against scrap growth:

| Scrap growth | H2-DRI | Scrap-EAF | BF-BOF | 2050 scrap use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.496 | 0.056 | 0.187 | 37 Mt |
| 0.04 | 0.418 | 0.167 | 0.149 | 99 Mt |
| 0.08 | 0.223 | 0.440 | 0.133 | 253 Mt |
| 0.10 | 0.110 | 0.635 | 0.120 | 394 Mt |

Hydrogen's share falls from 50% to 11% as scrap grows, while the incumbent
blast-furnace route stays nearly flat at 12–19%. **The two low-carbon routes
substitute for each other, not for the incumbent**, which retreats only to a
floor. That is the mechanism behind the frontier: the levers are
interchangeable because they compete for the same job, and the target binds
because neither displaces the incumbent below its floor on its own.

Deployment capacity constrains hydrogen with diminishing force. Raising the
electrolyser ramp from low to medium adds 3.86 percentage points of hydrogen
share; medium to high adds 1.40 (2050 H2-DRI capacity: 119.6, 154.1, 169.6
Mt). Hydrogen is **deployment-limited at a low ramp and approaching
cost-limited at a high one** — a qualification that should travel with any
claim about which constrains uptake.

## Where infeasibility concentrates

Of 16,285 infeasible scenarios, 50.7% sit at the 1.6 target, 32.6% at 1.8 and
16.8% at 2.0. By hydrogen debut the distribution runs 3.4% / 17.1% / 34.9% /
44.6% from 2030 to 2045; by scrap growth, 30.1% at zero growth falling to 0.9%
at 0.10/yr. Infeasibility is not scattered — it is concentrated in a single
corner of the design, defined by a late hydrogen programme and a weak scrap
supply, and it is that corner rather than any individual lever that the policy
conclusion rests on.

## Note on the corrected ramp

These feasibility rates were produced under a corrected electrolyser ramp
ceiling. The previous formulation let the ceiling decay after its crest, which
allowed a *later* hydrogen programme to out-build an earlier one; correcting it
moved 3,272 scenarios into feasibility and none out, and made cost monotone in
the debut year across all 19,265 paired comparisons. The diagnosis belongs in
methods; feasibility results should note only that they postdate the fix.
